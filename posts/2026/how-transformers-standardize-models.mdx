## Three inches east

On May 31, 1886, the sound of thousands of sledgehammers striking railroad spikes rang across the southern United States. Over two days, work crews pulled the spikes from 11,500 miles of the west rail, shifted it three inches east, and spiked it down again. Laid end to end, that is enough track to cross the United States nearly four times over.

When railroads were first built in the U.S., the gauge, the distance between two rails, varied from line to line. The New York and Erie Railroad ran a 6 ft gauge to carry larger, more powerful locomotives, while the South Carolina Canal and Rail Road ran a 5 ft gauge that spread across the South until it was the region's common track.

Railroads with different gauges could not connect. At a break of gauge, where one line met another of a different width, everything had to come off. Passengers changed trains and freight was transloaded onto the next one, slowly and at a cost.

Some towns built their economy on these breaks. Erie, Pennsylvania, sat where a 6 ft line met a 4 ft 10 in one, so anything traveling from Buffalo to Cleveland had to change trains twice. Delayed passengers ate in Erie's restaurants and slept in its hotels. Local crews made a living moving freight between the trains. So in 1853, when the railroads announced they would align the gauges, Erie rioted. The townspeople tore up the new track and destroyed the bridges.

After the Civil War, as trade between North and South grew, so did the cost of transloading at every break of gauge. In 1886 the South converted its railroads to 4 ft 9 in, close enough to the standard gauge the North used that trains could run straight through, and from then on a freight train loaded in Atlanta could ride the same rail all the way to Chicago. After decades of breaks, the country's railroads were finally one network. That was one way to get there.

## Changing the wheels in motion

Not everyone moved their railroads. Spain uses a variable gauge system that lets a train move between gauges without stopping. Its older network runs on the wide Iberian gauge it shares with Portugal, while its newer high-speed lines use standard gauge to connect with France.

Spain's Talgo trains ride on variable gauge axles. When a train passes over the gauge changer, guide rails take its weight while the wheels are unlocked from the axle. With the wheels free, the widening or narrowing track slides them to their new position on the axle. A second set of guide rails locks each wheel in place, and the train rolls onto the new gauge, never once stopping while its wheels are reset beneath its passengers.

These are two different ways to standardize. One forces everyone to move their rails, and the other makes crossing the boundary free. Machine learning has the same break of gauge, because every lab ships its models in its own format, and Hugging Face Transformers is the library much of the open-model world loads those models through. Its dynamic weight converter took the second way across, standardizing the model ecosystem without asking anyone to move their rails. Before it, meeting each model in its own format was a tax the whole ecosystem paid, one hand-written script at a time. Someone still has to map a genuinely new format by hand the first time, but once is the whole price now.

## Every lab lays its own track

No model lab chooses its checkpoint format to be difficult. A checkpoint is the file a trained model ships in, a collection of named arrays of numbers called weights. Some railroads picked a wider track to carry larger locomotives or bulkier goods, others laid a narrower one because it was cheaper to build, and every gauge made sense to whoever laid it. Labs make the same kind of choice, laying out a checkpoint to fit the way the model was trained and the hardware it was born on.

A checkpoint can part ways with the standard along a few axes. The model can be divided into pieces differently, the pieces can be named differently, the numbers inside them can be arranged differently, and they can be stored at different precisions. Each choice is reasonable on its own, and any two together are enough to make one lab's loader useless on another's file.

Start with how the model is divided into pieces. Mixtral-8x22B from Mistral is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) model. Instead of one big network, it is built from eight smaller ones called experts, and a router sends each word to just a couple of them. The checkpoint stores the experts the way the model is built, as eight separate sets of weights, one per expert. A model that keeps its experts in a single fused block, the way Transformers does, cannot read that arrangement without first gathering the eight back together.

Then there is what those pieces are called. Qwen2-57B-A14B, another MoE, this one from Qwen, carries 57B total parameters but activates only 14B of them. Qwen made the same per-expert choice as Mistral, but followed the Llama naming convention instead, calling its weight matrices `gate_proj`, `up_proj`, and `down_proj`. The idea is the same and the names are not, so a loader built for one checkpoint cannot find the weights in the other. DeepSeek-V4 diverges the same way for a different reason. It ships its attention weights under the names from its paper, `wq_a`, `wq_b`, `wkv`, and `wo_a`, convenient for anyone who read the paper and a mystery to every loader that learned a different vocabulary.

Naming aside, the numbers themselves can be arranged differently. Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B ships its experts pre-fused into 3D tensors, then flips the last two axes. Where everyone else lays `(experts, ffn, hidden)`, it lays `(experts, hidden, ffn)`, because its training stack read them the other way around. The names can line up perfectly and the file still won't load, because the same numbers are sitting in the wrong order.

The weights can also arrive at a different precision. DeepSeek-V3 ships its weights compressed to FP8 with 128x128 block scales, because it was trained that way on H800 GPUs. Storing it in FP8 rather than the usual bf16 halves both the file and the download, turning what would be a 1.3TB checkpoint into 671GB, and the weights mean the same thing. A loader expecting full precision reads noise though, because the numbers only make sense next to their scales.

Working with any of these models used to mean running a one-off conversion script, lifting a checkpoint out of its native format and into the one Transformers expects. The script ran once, and the converted weights went up on the Hugging Face Hub, the public repository the ecosystem downloads its models from. That copy became, in effect, the Transformers format. Every other tool in the ecosystem then adapted to that shape, and the places it didn't are where the ecosystem fragmented. That was standardization of the first kind, the kind that makes everyone move their rails. Someone still had to write each script, and the pile grew with every new model. Transformers accumulated more than 300 of them.

The dynamic weight converter retires the one-off script, and with it the idea that any one format has to be canonical. Arthur Zucker, one of the library's core maintainers, landed it in November 2025. One file, `conversion_mapping.py`, now speaks for 164 model types, and only 76 of them lay any track of their own. The other 88 take a single line each. The line points the model at an existing one whose format it already matches, so it reuses that conversion instead of carrying its own. Most new mixture-of-experts models just declare that they load like Mixtral or Qwen2.

{`
  _MODEL_TO_CONVERSION_PATTERN = {
      # Mixtral-style MoE
      "minimax": "mixtral",
      "minimax_m2": "mixtral",
      # Qwen2-style MoE
      "afmoe": "qwen2_moe",
      "deepseek_v2": "qwen2_moe",
      "deepseek_v3": "qwen2_moe",
      "deepseek_v32": "qwen2_moe",
      ...
    }
`}
</Snippet>

The old scripts ran to more than 100,000 lines all told. `conversion_mapping.py` does the same work in 1,616. A script was a transloading crew waiting at the end of every journey. The converter changes the gauge in motion instead, and nothing has to stop.

## Nobody touches the rails

Each array in a checkpoint has a name and a shape. The name says what each array is, and the shape says how its numbers are laid out. Loading a model means renaming every array to Transformers' standard names and restacking the numbers into the standard shape. The dynamic converter does this while the file streams in, one array at a time, the way the Talgo changes gauge without stopping the train.

Follow one of Mixtral's expert weights as it arrives.

<Snippet>
{`
  model.layers.7.block_sparse_moe.experts.3.w1.weight # Mixtral
  model.layers.7.mlp.experts.gate_up_proj             # Transformers
`}
</Snippet>

First, the converter rewrites the name. Mixtral stores this part of the model under `block_sparse_moe`, and Transformers expects it under `mlp`.

Then the converter works out where the renamed array belongs and which of its neighbors it joins. Mixtral's eight experts arrive as eight separate arrays, and Transformers wants them as a single stacked block, so this one is held until the other seven arrive. The converter reads each array from disk only when it needs it, and the rest keep streaming past untouched.

Once the last of the eight is in hand, the numbers are restacked. A short chain of small operations (`MergeModulelist`, `Concatenate`) does the work. One stacks the eight separate experts into a single block, another fuses the two matrices Mixtral keeps apart, `gate` and `up`, into one. This is where the gauge actually changes. Each operation is small and chains together in any order, so a short list of them describes a whole model where a script used to run to hundreds of lines.

The finished block is mounted on the running model, and the converter moves straight on to the next array. The model is built in the act of loading it, with no separate conversion pass and no intermediate copy written to disk and read back in.

The journey runs the other way too. Two weeks after the converter landed, Cyril Vallez, another of the library's maintainers, gave every operation an inverse, so a model loaded into the standard layout can be saved back in its native one. The stack becomes a split, and the merge becomes a cut. Because it works in both directions, no lab is ever forced to re-lay its rails.

## The freight that follows

Standardization means any model loads anywhere, with no conversion step between formats. The most immediate gain is how fast new models arrive.

Switch Transformers was the first mixture-of-experts model in the library. Between it and the converter, Transformers added 31 more over three years, about one a month, each with its own weight-handling code. In the seven months since, it has added 19 more, one every 11 days. Of those, 13 needed no new code, most just a line pointing at Mixtral or Qwen2. The industry's turn toward mixture-of-experts is bigger than any one library, so the converter did not start the rush. What it changed is the cost of keeping up, and a new MoE that once took a few hundred lines now usually takes one. They keep arriving faster.

The deeper gain is what the standard layout unlocks once a model is loaded.

DeepSeek-V3 is stored compressed in FP8. Each block of numbers travels with a small scale value that says how to expand it back to full precision, and that pairing is fragile. When the converter stacks the experts into one block, every scale has to ride along and stay matched to the exact numbers it belongs to, or the decompression turns to noise. Keeping them aligned used to take a dedicated script. The converter does it by slotting a decompression step into the front of the same operation chain and letting the scales travel beside their weights, so the merge that follows runs exactly as it does for any other model. The whole FP8 path is one line in the conversion mapping pointing DeepSeek-V3 at Qwen2's pattern, plus that decompression step, and there is no DeepSeek-specific code anywhere.

The standard layout is also what lets a model spread across many GPUs. Stacked into one block, the experts divide evenly. DeepSeek-V3's 256 land 32 to a GPU across eight of them, each one holding either a few whole experts (expert parallelism) or a thin slice of every expert (tensor parallelism). A scattered pile of separate pieces won't divide that cleanly. The same stacked block is the shape the fastest math routines want. They multiply all the experts in a single batched step instead of looping over them one at a time.

The biggest gain barely touches Transformers itself, and goes instead to everyone building around it. Without a common shape, every tool needs its own way to read every other tool's models, and the number of those paths grows faster than the number of tools. A shared standard needs only one. A training framework like Unsloth and an inference engine like vLLM no longer have to meet each other halfway. They meet at the same model definitions instead, and a checkpoint passes between them without being rewritten. Each tool aligns once and is then compatible with every model that adopts the standard. What makes this a standard rather than a Transformers feature is that none of it requires running Transformers at all. A model only has to match the definitions, or import them, and it joins the ecosystem the moment it does.

Every one of these gains follows from the same fact. The weights now share a gauge.

That gauge still has to be laid by hand the first time. The 76 model types that carry conversion code of their own are the ones no existing rails fit, and a genuinely new format has to be mapped before anything can ride in behind it. The converter doesn't make the first crossing free, only every crossing after it.

## Just another progress bar

The converter standardizes the other way, the free way. Unlike the Erie Gauge War, which made winners and losers, it makes no enemies. Nobody's rails get torn up, and no lab is forced to move. Labs go on uploading checkpoints in whatever format suits them, and the reconciling happens at the boundary, the moment Transformers loads the model.

The free way is only available because of what the rails are made of. Moving steel takes labor and runs one direction, so a railroad that picks a different gauge is mostly stuck with it. A conversion is cheap and every one has an inverse, so a weight can cross between two formats and cross straight back. Standardization can be frictionless only when translation is cheap and reversible, and steel never quite managed it. Spain came closest, and even then only by moving the crossing onto the train instead of the track.

Railroads are not the only network to have split this way. Japan's power grid has run at two frequencies since the 1890s, when Tokyo bought its first generators from Germany and Osaka bought its own from the United States, and the east has run at 50Hz and the west at 60Hz ever since. Nobody re-laid that track either. Frequency converter stations sit at the boundary and translate power from one side to the other, but electrical translation is expensive, so the crossing stayed narrow. When the 2011 earthquake shut down power stations in the east, the west had electricity to spare, and the converters could pass about one gigawatt of it, roughly a tenth of what the east was missing. Each boundary gets as much standardization as its translation can afford, and weights are the cheapest cargo yet, because they are only numbers, and numbers can be renamed and restacked for almost nothing.

In 1886, changing the gauge took thousands of sledgehammers and a coordinated two days across 11,500 miles of track. Load a 1T-parameter FP8 MoE checkpoint across eight GPUs today, and the converter does all of it without anyone swinging a hammer or getting off the train. It is just another progress bar, and the ordinariness is the achievement. The next model like it should cross for free.
